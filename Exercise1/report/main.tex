\section{Introduction}

In this assignment we compare two methods for estimating optical flow: Lucas-Kanade and Horn-Schunck. We test them
on different types of images and analyze their performance. 

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Testing on different types of images}

are visible in figure~\ref{fig:noise}. HS produces smooth flow due to the global regularization term, while LK
appears smooth here, because random noise has gradients in all directions, which means it can calculate flow everywhere.

We also tested the methods on other types of images. In figure~\ref{fig:translation} we can see that both
methods work well when the small motion assumption holds. They both show motion concentrated around
object boundaries and textured regions. HS is visibly smoother than LK, which is due to the global smoothness constraint. 
In the next example, seen in figure~\ref{fig:translation_fail}
LK estimates motion correctly only in the corners, where gradients exist in both spatial directions (for the rest they are 0). 
Along the edges motion is partially recovered, but it is not reliable. HS produces a smooth flow field,
but it's not accurate. It shows motion everywhere and in random directions in some cases. This is the result of the aperture problem, where
motion cannot be uniquely determined along edges.


The last example (figure~\ref{fig:collision_fail}) shows a non uniform motion due to perspective effects (the camera moving
closer to the toy car). Both methods estimate the overall flow correctly by showing motion away from the center.
Again the difference is that HS produces a smoother flow field, while LK shows more details but also more noise.

\subsection{Reliability of Lucas-Kanade}

In LK flow is computed only where the structure tensor has strong eigen values. To detect this we 
can use the Harris response detector. 
In figure~\ref{fig:harris_comparison} we plotted the magnitude of the flow computed by LK with and without Harris-based
filtering. We can see that on the right the LK produces small noisy vectors in uniform areas, while on the left the
harris filtering zeroed out pixels that the algorithm cannot reliably compute flow for (the motion has been preserved only
in regions where the gradients are strong in both directions). 

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width = 0.2\textwidth]{magnitude_harris.png}
        \caption{Comparison of LK with (left) and without (right) Harris detector.}\label{fig:harris_comparison}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Parameters analysis}

The LK method has a window size parameter which controls the trade-off between noise and detail. Larger windows may
over smooth and lose fine details (as they average gradients over a larger region), while smaller windows can be more sensitive to noise. An example for 
this can be seen in figure~\ref{fig:windowSize_comparison}.
There is also the gaussian smoothing sigma parameter which controls how much the image is smoothed 
before computing the flow. A larger sigma can help reduce noise but can also blur important features. 
Lastly there is the harris threshold which if too high can discard good flow, while if too low can keep 
unreliable flow vectors.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.22\textwidth}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{lk_windowSize.png}
        \caption{LK method with window size 15.}\label{fig:windowSize}
    \end{subfigure}
    \hfill
    \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.22\textwidth}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{lk_windowSizeSmall.png}
        \caption{LK method with window size 3.}\label{fig:windowSizeSmall}
    \end{subfigure}
    \caption{Comparison of Lucas-Kanade with change in parameter.}\label{fig:windowSize_comparison}
\end{figure}

The HS method has the smoothness regularization parameter $\lambda$. It's a weighing factor which decides how much importance
do we give to the brightness constancy constraint and how much to the smoothness constraint. A larger $\lambda$ will produce
smoother flow fields, but can also lose details (as it enforces stronger smoothness). 
On the other hand a smaller $\lambda$ can capture more details but 
is more sensitive to noise. The difference is seen in figure~\ref{fig:lambda_comparison}. With $\lambda = 0.01$ the 
flow is very noisy because it prioritizes detail over smoothness, while with $\lambda = 10$ the flow is smoother but less accurate
as it over-smooths the boundaries. The HS method also has the number of iterations parameter which controls how many times
the flow is updated. More iterations leads to better convergence but also increases computational cost. 

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.2\textwidth]{HS_diffLambda.png}
        \caption{HS with $\lambda = 0.01$ on the left and $\lambda = 10$ on the right.}\label{fig:lambda_comparison}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Runtime and initialization}
The LK method is generally faster than HS since it computes flow only at certain points and uses a local window. The HS
method uses iterative optimization which can be computationally expensive, especially for larger images. The runtime for both
can be affected by the choice of parameters, such as window size for LK and number of iterations for HS. We tested the
runtime for both methods on image pairs from the lab2 dataset. The results are shown in table~\ref{tab:runtime}. We can
see that LK is significantly faster than HS. However initializing HS with the output of LK can speed up convergence
and reduce runtime.

The speed and performance of HS with and without harris detector is visualized in~\ref{fig:error_convergence}.
The error is calculated as the sum of the data term and the smoothness term. Using LK initialization leads to faster
convergence as it starts closer to the optimal solution.

\subsection{Pyramidal Lucas-Kanade}
The pyramidal approach reduces large displacements by operating at a lower resolutions where motion appears smaller and then 
adjusting the estimate back to higher resolutions.
The standard LK method works well only for small motions. If an object moves fast, the small motion assumption is violated. A pyramidal
implementation addressed this issue by creating a pyramid of images at different scales. In figure~\ref{fig:pyramidal_comparison} we can
see how the standard LK fails to capture the motion of a fast moving object, while the pyramidal implementation
is much more smooth and captures the motion much better. 

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.22\textwidth}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{NOTpyramidal_.png}
        \caption{Lucas-Kanade method.}\label{fig:notpyramidal}
    \end{subfigure}
    \hfill
    \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.22\textwidth}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{pyramidal_LK.png}
        \caption{Pyramidal LK method.}\label{fig:pyramidal}
    \end{subfigure}
    \caption{Comparison of LK and Pyramidal LK.}\label{fig:pyramidal_comparison}
\end{figure}

Running LK multiple times at the same scale is not a substitute for the pyramidal approach. To test this we ran 5
iterations of LK at the same scale but used warped images based on the previous flow estimate. We used two images from the
provided collision image set that were 10 frames apart. In figure~\ref{fig:iterative_vs_pyramidal} we compare angle magnitudes of the flow
estimation for the basic LK, iterative LK and pyramidal LK. We can see that the iterative approach slightly improves
on the basic LK, but still fails to capture larger displacements (inconsisten colors in the plot, some white backgroung meaning zero motion
or noise). So it confirms that the pyramid structure is the only robust way, as it has uniform colors around the
expanding objects showing that it captures the consistent direction away from the center well.

\section{Conclusion}

Lucas-Kanade is fast and captures local motion accurately, but it can struggle with large displacements. Horn-Schunck
is can reduce noise and capture global motion, but it is computationally expensive and can blur motion boundaries. Adding Harris-based filtering
improves LK reliability, while initializing HS with LK output can speed up convergence. The pyramidal approach is
essential for handling large motions effectively. Overall, the choice of method depends on the task at hand.
